Executive guide

Digital Sovereignty

Understand where control sits, which dependencies matter and how the business can recover if a provider, contract or operating arrangement changes.

Public guidance. No contact details are needed to read this guide. Use it to frame a decision, then validate the requirements of your own environment.

Define the control you need

Digital sovereignty is a practical control discussion. Start by identifying which business services, information and decisions require particular control arrangements. The answer should describe what the organisation must be able to govern, access, recover or move. A country name or a hosting location alone does not answer all of those questions.

Set the scope and the accountable business owner. Identify the decisions that need specialist legal, security or contractual review. This guide is a way to organise the discussion; it does not determine whether a particular arrangement satisfies legislation or an organisation's obligations.

Map jurisdiction and residency

Record the providers involved, their contractual roles and the jurisdictions relevant to the service. Ask where production data, replicas, backups, support records and recovery environments are located. Include subcontracted services where they materially affect the requirement. Keep confirmed facts separate from sales descriptions and assumptions.

Document the residency requirements that apply to each information class and business service. Assign an owner to maintain that record as providers, contracts and data flows change. Bring uncertain obligations to appropriately qualified reviewers before relying on a location claim in an investment decision.

Look at access and operating authority

Ask who can access the information and change the platform, including administrators, contractors, support staff and providers. Review how access is approved, time-limited, monitored and removed. For critical information, determine whether activity can be traced to an accountable person and whether material exceptions reach the right decision-maker.

Identify the difference between owning information and controlling the systems that make it usable. Consider administrative authority, keys, credentials, configuration, dependencies and the operating knowledge needed during a disruption. A paper right of access is incomplete if the organisation cannot exercise it when the service is under pressure.

Prove recoverability and exit assumptions

Define what a usable recovery or exit looks like for the business service. Include data format, completeness, configuration, application dependencies, licensing, support capability and the target environment. Establish which steps depend on the current provider and which can be performed independently.

Test a representative export and restore rather than relying only on a documented right to export. Record the result and limitations. If the test exposes missing skills, incompatible formats or unavailable dependencies, treat those as explicit remediation tasks. Repeat the exercise when material conditions change.

Govern the trade-offs

Compare options against the specific controls required. More direct control may also require greater operating capability, cost or responsibility. A provider service may be suitable when responsibilities and evidence are clear. The aim is an arrangement the business can govern and operate, with its limitations understood.

Maintain a decision and exception record. State which dependencies are accepted, who accepted them, why the arrangement is appropriate and when it must be reviewed. Ensure the record connects to procurement, access governance, resilience planning and changes to critical platforms.

Prepare an executive control review

Bring five items to the review: a provider and jurisdiction map; a data-location register; an access and authority map; a recovery and exit evidence record; and a list of unresolved decisions. Each gap should have an owner and a clear next action. Do not turn missing evidence into an unsupported claim that the arrangement is either safe or non-compliant.

The useful outcome is a prioritised control plan. Decide what can be retained, what needs validation, what must change and which questions require qualified advice. Review progress against evidence of operating control and recovery, rather than the number of policy documents produced.

Discuss your next decision

The assessment provides an initial, self-reported view. A follow-up should validate the relevant answers, agree the outcome and define the scope before any commitment.
